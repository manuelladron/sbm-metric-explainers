Coverage

Coverage asks a blunt but important question: did the method furnish the room with the right inventory? It is deliberately not a geometry metric. It does not care whether the bed is centered perfectly or whether a sink is three inches farther left. It cares whether the room program is there.

Coverageinventory satisfaction, weighted by importance, minus unsupported extras

In the current benchmark, essential items carry weight 1.0, optional items carry weight 0.5, missing items are penalized more than overfill, and unsupported extras subtract a separate penalty term.

The Metric In One Formula

Coverage = 100 × ( ((Σ wi si + Σ wg sg) / (Σ wi + Σ wg)) − pextra )

The benchmark begins with a room-type specification. A master bedroom, for example, requires one bed, two end tables, and one storage piece, with television optional. Each required or optional item gets a score based on the predicted count, and some room functions are expressed as requirement groups rather than single categories. A full bath can satisfy bathing with a tub or a shower; a kitchen can satisfy cooking with a range or with oven plus cooktop.

Name Normalization Matters

A fair coverage metric cannot rely on exact spelling. Different methods say King, bed, nightstand, endtable, or cabinet. The benchmark normalizes all of those to canonical categories before scoring.

In the current master-bedroom semantics, cabinet, base_cabinet, and tv_cabinet all count as the storage requirement and are normalized to dresser. That matters because otherwise methods would be punished for naming conventions rather than for genuine inventory failures.

What Coverage Captures, And What It Doesn’t

Coverage is intentionally blind to circulation. A room can score well on inventory and still be blocked. It can also score poorly while remaining easy to move through. That is why the benchmark keeps navigability and overlap-clearance as separate metrics.

Coverage answers “what should be there?”

It checks counts, alternatives, semantic normalization, and unsupported inventory.

It does not answer “can I use it?”

That question belongs to navigability and overlap-clearance, which are explicitly geometric.

That separation is deliberate

The benchmark becomes easier to interpret when each metric isolates one failure mode instead of mixing everything together.
